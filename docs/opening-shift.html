Open a shift with a known float

Separate cash custody, denomination checks, equipment readiness and exception recording into a calm opening routine.

Mara Ellis · fictional editorial persona
Published 11 October 2026 · desk-based guidance

Practical decisions for a cash checkout.

The complete overviewMethod and limitationsContact availability

The opening routine establishes two different facts: the checkout equipment is usable, and the person taking custody knows what cash is present. Neither fact implies the other. A drawer can open correctly with an incorrect float, while a correctly counted float can sit inside a checkout whose printer is disconnected. Keeping those facts separate makes the routine easier to teach and makes a later discrepancy easier to interpret.

This guide describes a hypothetical business procedure, not a rule for every jurisdiction or a substitute for your organization's controls. Use approved counting locations, access rules and record systems. The fictional editorial persona has not tested a particular cash drawer or point-of-sale setup. For the overall equipment choice, start with the main checkout guide; this page is about the first few minutes of a shift and the information they should leave behind.

Establish custody before the first transaction

Identify which till and station are being handed over, who is releasing custody and who is accepting it. In a small operation, that may involve one cashier retrieving a prepared float under a manager's procedure. In a shared operation, it may involve a documented handover between two workers. The important point is that the responsibility change has a clear time and a clear object. “The drawer was there when I arrived” is an observation, not a custody record.

Keep identifiers practical. A station number and insert label can help distinguish several similar drawers, but the label must correspond to the record people actually use. Do not rely on color alone, which may be hard to distinguish or describe. Avoid putting sensitive access information on a public-facing label. The operational record belongs in the approved business system, not in the visible till or on a note that a customer can read.

Before accepting custody, follow the required check for unexpected contents, damaged seals if your procedure uses them, or missing keys and inserts. Do not assume a seal proves the amount inside. Where your policy calls for a count, the count remains necessary. If the incoming cashier finds a discrepancy, record and escalate it before normal trading begins. That protects the record from an avoidable ambiguity about whether the difference existed at handover.

Count denominations, not just a grand total

An opening float is useful only if its composition supports the change you expect to give. A correct total composed entirely of large notes can be operationally unsuitable. Count each denomination using the method required by your business, then total the count separately. Keep the physical groups in a stable order so a recount can be performed without rearranging everything. Do not mix an unverified delivery of change with the accepted float before it has been recorded.

Use a denomination sheet or approved digital record whose meaning is clear. If a field asks for the number of pieces, enter pieces rather than their monetary value. If it asks for value, use the correct units. A hurried change from one notation to another can create a false total. For coin rolls or prepared packs, follow the organization's verification policy rather than assuming the wrapper establishes the contents or inventing a new shortcut.

Record the actual observed count, even when it differs from the planned float. A planned amount is not a count. Retain the first count and a documented recount if required, rather than erasing the first entry after discovering a mistake. When the discrepancy is resolved by adding or removing money, record the authorized movement separately. The cashier should begin with a known amount and a visible explanation of how it reached the drawer.

Match the float to the day's change pattern

A float should reflect transaction patterns rather than a permanently memorized arrangement. Review which notes and coins run low during ordinary trading, when larger tender tends to arrive, and whether a nearby event changes the expected volume. Use actual business records where available; do not guess customer behavior from the category of shop. The goal is a sensible distribution that reduces interruptions without storing unnecessary excess cash at the checkout.

Changing the float needs authorization and documentation. A cashier should not quietly borrow from another station because its coin cup looks fuller. That turns a change shortage into two stations with uncertain balances. Define where replacement change comes from, who can approve it and how each transfer is recorded. If two tills exchange denominations without changing their total amounts, the movement can still matter for operational records and custody.

Treat tips, coupons, vouchers and personal money according to explicit rules. They do not belong in whatever spare compartment happens to be empty. Some systems record cash tips or other adjustments, while others rely on a separate procedure. Understand the configured method and keep money streams distinct enough to reconcile. The cash-close guide explains how those distinctions affect the expected closing figure rather than merely the physical arrangement of the till.

Check the workstation without making a sale

Look for obvious physical obstructions, a strained lead, loose mounting or a drawer left in an unexpected mechanical state. Do not reach into moving equipment or disassemble it as part of opening. A cashier's readiness check is a limited observation, not a technical inspection. Where the manufacturer provides a supported opening test and the business permits it, confirm that the drawer responds through the intended point-of-sale and printer path before serving customers.

Keep testing distinct from transactions. Do not charge a real card, create a real sale or submit the same transaction repeatedly simply to make the drawer open. Use an approved training or test route only after understanding its effect on reports. A setting called “test” in one context may still leave a record requiring attention elsewhere. If there is no suitable approved action, ask the responsible person for a commissioning procedure instead of improvising one.

Check receipt availability and the printer assignment separately. A drawer attached to one printer will not necessarily respond when a different printer is selected. Note the correct station and supported route in private documentation. The connection-path guide helps identify the chain, while the counter-fit guide gives a physical checklist. A working screen alone is not proof that the whole checkout is ready for cash transactions.

Arrange the till for a predictable first sale

Use the same denomination order across shifts where your business policy allows it. Familiar placement reduces the need to search, especially when the cashier is interrupted. Keep note clips unobstructed and coin cups clear enough to distinguish contents. Do not overfill a compartment so that notes interfere with closing or spill behind the insert. If the drawer cannot hold a useful float without crowding, investigate the layout or equipment choice rather than treating the spill risk as normal.

Give paperwork a defined home. Paid-out receipts, deposit records, coupons and transaction slips may need different treatment; combining them with loose money makes the closing count harder. A media slot can be useful only if the receiving compartment is included in the authorized close procedure. Explain what may be inserted there and who retrieves it. Hidden cash does not stop being part of the reconciliation because it is temporarily out of sight.

Clear personal items from the till and nearby working surface. Phones, drinks and loose stationery can obstruct movement or introduce contamination and spills. Store essential supplies where they can be reached without leaving the drawer unattended. Our drawer-care guide discusses appropriate cleaning boundaries; avoid spraying cleaner into compartments or assuming any visible metal surface tolerates the same chemical treatment as the countertop.

Define the exception before a queue forms

If the count is wrong or the drawer does not respond, the incoming cashier needs a short, approved escalation route. Name the responsible role, identify the information to capture and state whether trading may begin under a contingency procedure. Do not leave the worker to choose between hiding the issue and delaying every customer without guidance. A controlled exception is part of readiness, not a sign that the routine has failed.

Capture observations before changing the arrangement. Note the station, time, actual count or equipment behavior, and any visible recent change. Avoid blaming the previous shift based on a discrepancy alone. An opening difference can arise from an earlier count, an unrecorded movement or a documentation error. The opening cashier's task is to preserve a useful record and follow the authorized next step, not to invent an explanation that makes the numbers comfortable.

Manual opening requires its own policy. The key can bypass an electronic opening path, so possession and use should be controlled. Do not place an unrestricted spare beside the terminal for convenience. If the business permits an outage route, record the access and relevant cash movements as required. The shared-checkout guide covers custody during unusual access and explains why a shared login is not a substitute for knowing who actually handled the till.

Hand the routine to people in plain language

A short opening procedure should describe visible actions and the records they create. “Verify the float” is too vague for a new cashier; “count each denomination in the approved record, compare with the planned amount, and report a difference before the first sale” is teachable. Pair the written steps with a supervised demonstration using a training arrangement. Let the learner explain the difference between counting money and confirming equipment behavior.

Teach interruptions explicitly. If someone speaks to the cashier halfway through a count, the worker should know whether to restart the affected group or use another approved method. Relying on memory after an interruption creates unnecessary uncertainty. Keep uncounted, counted and exception items visibly separate during the process. Make it possible to stop safely without leaving cash exposed or losing track of the last verified denomination.

Review the routine when equipment, permissions or float policy changes. A printer replacement can alter the opening route; a new shared station can alter custody; a different trade pattern can alter denomination needs. Retain the current procedure where authorized staff can find it and remove obsolete versions from active use. A dependable opening does not require elaborate language. It requires a known float, a usable workstation, a clear owner and an honest record of anything that remains unresolved.
