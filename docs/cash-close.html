Close cash with a traceable count

Build a closing record that distinguishes physical cash, expected cash, transfers, refunds and unresolved differences.

Mara Ellis · fictional editorial persona
Published 11 October 2026 · desk-based guidance

Practical decisions for a cash checkout.

The complete overviewMethod and limitationsContact availability

Closing a cash drawer is not the same as counting a day's sales. The physical money includes a starting float and may have been changed by refunds, paid-outs, change transfers and cash moved to another controlled location. Sales reports can include payment methods that never entered the drawer. A useful close compares like with like and preserves enough detail for another authorized person to follow the result without reconstructing the entire shift from memory.

This is a workflow explanation, not accounting, tax or legal advice. Apply your business's policies and ask a qualified adviser about the treatment of specific transactions. The examples are hypothetical and the publication's editor is fictional; no product testing or commercial bookkeeping service is represented here. The checkout planning article covers equipment selection. This guide focuses on the record produced after the final cash transaction and before custody moves elsewhere.

Define the expected figure before looking for a difference

Find out exactly what the relevant point-of-sale report calls “expected cash” or its equivalent. Does it include the opening float? Does it subtract recorded cash refunds? Does it include cash tips or paid-outs? Does it account for documented cash removed during trading? Different configurations can present these items differently. Read the current documentation and your business procedure rather than assuming a report title establishes its arithmetic.

A conceptual reconciliation starts with opening cash, adds recorded cash received, subtracts recorded cash given back or removed, and includes authorized adjustments with the correct direction. That description is not an instruction to calculate a second figure beside a report that already performs the adjustments. Applying the same refund or transfer twice creates a false discrepancy. Map each relevant item to the report or record where it is already represented before using the figure.

Keep payment methods distinct. A card sale contributes to sales but not to notes and coins inside the drawer. A mixed payment may contribute only its cash portion. A refund can use a different payment method from the original sale under permitted procedures, and its actual treatment needs to be understood. Do not use total revenue as the drawer target. The closing worker needs an expected cash figure whose components are explainable, not the largest number on the screen.

Freeze a clear transaction boundary

Decide when the station stops accepting transactions for the close. If one person continues taking cash while another counts the insert, the count can mix two periods. In a location that must remain open, use the approved handover or spare-till procedure to establish a boundary. Record which station, shift and till the close concerns, as well as the time responsibility changes. The process should let a later reader distinguish closing money from the next person's opening money.

Resolve transaction state before handling the cash. If a payment was interrupted, check its recorded status through the authorized process rather than resubmitting it to make the drawer open. Duplicate submissions can create additional charges or records. Where an outage procedure was used, identify the manual records that need reconciliation. Do not assume every handwritten sale has already reached the software or that entering it later should repeat the original payment action.

Make custody explicit while the till is moved. An insert carried to a counting location remains someone’s responsibility, and a drawer body left at the counter may still contain slips or money in another compartment. Follow the organization’s approved handling procedure. The shared-checkout guide describes how a handover can identify the exact point when responsibility changes instead of relying on a vague statement that the shop was closed.

Count the physical contents in a stable sequence

Separate notes, coins and other items using the approved denomination order. Count one group at a time, record it, and keep counted groups distinct from uncounted groups. If you are interrupted, use the procedure for restarting the affected count rather than estimating where you stopped. A quiet, appropriately controlled location helps, but a good sequence also makes interruption recovery possible. Speed is less useful than a count that can be reproduced.

Do not make the physical count conform to an expected result. Record what you observe first, then compare. Some businesses use a blind count so the cashier does not see the expected figure beforehand; whether that is appropriate is a policy decision, not something this page can prescribe. In every case, keep the original observation and any subsequent recount identifiable. Erasing the first count conceals whether a later correction came from finding more money or correcting arithmetic.

Check all designated compartments. A media slot or under-till space may hold permitted deposits, slips or notes that are easy to overlook. Inspect only the areas authorized by the business and the equipment instructions; do not dismantle the drawer mechanism as part of a routine count. Keep vouchers, coupons, checks and other non-cash items separate according to policy. Their face values are not automatically interchangeable with physical cash in the closing record.

Separate the next float from the counted result

The amount reserved for the next opening is a movement made after the total physical cash has been established. If you remove that float before recording the total, another person may misunderstand the count as a deposit amount rather than the drawer contents. Label each figure by purpose: total counted cash, amount retained for the next float, and amount moved to the approved destination. Do not rely on a column heading that means different things to different shifts.

Count the proposed next float by denomination as well as total. A reserved amount can be correct but poorly distributed for the next shift's likely change needs. Obtain any authorized replacement change through the documented route. Do not quietly exchange cash between stations after their counts have been finalized. Even when an exchange preserves each total, it can undermine denomination records or make the physical handover difficult to reproduce.

Record the custody and destination of the prepared float and the remaining money under policy. This guide does not designate a checkout drawer, bag or cupboard as secure storage. Security needs a premises-specific assessment and approved handling controls. The opening-shift guide explains the next worker's responsibility: accept a known counted amount rather than treating a prepared package or previous signature as proof of its contents.

Investigate a difference without rewriting history

Start with a recount and a check of denomination arithmetic if the approved procedure calls for them. Then look for documentary explanations: an omitted opening amount, a recorded paid-out, a transfer attached to a different station, or a refund represented twice. Review specific records rather than changing transactions until a balance looks satisfactory. A discrepancy is information about the relationship between two records; it is not by itself proof of theft or carelessness.

Keep investigation separate from approval. The closing cashier can preserve observations and supply the relevant paperwork, while a designated manager or other role decides what happens next. That distinction prevents a worker from feeling forced to create a convenient explanation before leaving. Describe unresolved differences honestly, including the original count, expected figure, recount and relevant observations. Avoid accusatory language unsupported by the record.

Never add personal money to conceal a shortage or remove an overage to make the drawer match. Do not backdate a cash movement or create a fictitious sale. The business needs a truthful record to identify training, system or control problems. Employment, accounting and legal questions about discrepancies require appropriate advice. A practical procedure provides a clear escalation path rather than expecting a cashier to settle those questions alone at the end of a tiring shift.

Make paperwork survive the handover

Attach or associate permitted supporting records with the close in the approved system. Useful identifiers include the station, shift period, till identity and the relevant report reference. Store records according to the business's retention and access rules. Do not put customer payment details, full card numbers, unnecessary personal data or access credentials into an informal closing notebook. A record can be detailed without being a collection of sensitive information.

If the business uses signatures or acknowledgments, explain what each person is acknowledging. A witness to a physical count is not necessarily approving the accounting treatment of every transaction. An incoming cashier accepting a float is not taking responsibility for every earlier sale. Clear wording matters because the close can later be reviewed by someone who was not present. Capture the action observed, not an inflated assurance about the entire day.

Protect the records from accidental mixing. Several identical printouts placed beside several identical inserts can create a station mismatch. Use stable identifiers and follow the approved handling sequence. Where a document is corrected, preserve the correction trail rather than silently replacing it. The counter-fit guide discusses a counting workspace and insert clearance, because the physical ability to separate tills supports the clarity of the records.

End with equipment state and unresolved work

After the cash procedure is complete, leave the equipment in the state required by the manufacturer and business. That may include clearing authorized contents, closing or locking the drawer, checking for obstruction and securing keys. Do not infer a universal overnight position from a generic guide. Security policy and equipment instructions should determine the arrangement. Record an equipment problem separately so the next shift does not discover it only when a customer needs change.

A short close summary can identify what is complete, what remains unresolved and which role owns the next action. “Count recorded; transfer acknowledged; printer issue reported” is more useful than a blanket “all good” when one component is still faulty. Follow the connection-path guide for describing an electronic opening fault and the drawer-care guide for safe handling. Do not attempt a mechanical or electrical repair merely because the counter is now quiet.

Review recurring differences across periods using authorized business records. Patterns may reveal a confusing report, an unclear paid-out process or an impractical handover. They should inform a measured change to the procedure rather than a nightly improvisation. A dependable close ends with a traceable amount, a documented destination and an honest exception record. It leaves the next person a clear starting point instead of a drawer full of assumptions.
