Measure the counter with the drawer open

Plan the body, opening envelope, reach, cable route and installation access before fitting a cash drawer into a checkout counter.

Mara Ellis · fictional editorial persona
Published 11 October 2026 · desk-based guidance

Practical decisions for a cash checkout.

The complete overviewMethod and limitationsContact availability

The smallest drawer in a catalog is not necessarily the easiest drawer to use. Width and depth tell you where the closed box fits, but not where the cashier stands, how the till is reached or what the moving tray interrupts. A good physical plan treats the drawer as part of a workstation used by actual people. This guide gives you a practical survey method without claiming to certify workplace ergonomics, accessibility or structural mounting safety.

Start with the checkout tasks rather than a furniture diagram. Watch or simulate where bags, receipts, coins, returns and scanning equipment need to go. Do not use customer money for a layout trial. A cardboard model and empty work surface are enough to expose many obvious conflicts. The main planning article connects this physical survey with compatibility, cash custody and closing procedures; this guide concentrates on fit and access.

Measure the complete closed envelope

Find the manufacturer's dimension drawing for the exact model you are considering. Record body width, depth and height, then identify what the published dimensions include. A protruding front panel, key cylinder, mounting feet or connector can sit outside the apparent box. Some catalog descriptions round dimensions or omit the cable space. Use the detailed drawing and installation directions to establish the actual envelope rather than assuming every measurement describes the same boundary.

Measure the counter at the intended location, including underside braces, shelf edges, legroom and the rear surface. A wide cavity may narrow behind its visible opening. Inspect where the cable will leave the drawer and where it will meet the printer or interface. Leave the bend and connector clearance specified by the equipment manufacturer. A plug pressed against a wall is not a successful fit, even if the drawer itself can be pushed into the gap.

Write each measurement on a sketch with its source. “Opening width measured at front” and “rear brace clearance measured underneath” are more useful than an unexplained number. Photograph the empty installation area for private planning if allowed, avoiding customers or confidential material. When a supplier asks for a measurement, you should be able to explain the boundary you measured. This reduces the chance of ordering around a dimension that describes the wrong space.

Draw the opening envelope on the floor plan

The drawer's open position changes the cashier's route. Determine the manufacturer's required extension or opening clearance and mark it on the counter sketch. Include the space occupied by the tray and the space a person needs to reach it without being trapped against another surface. A front that clears the counter lip can still collide with a cupboard door, chair, waste bin or the worker behind the adjacent register.

Use a temporary cardboard box or taped outline to represent the moving portion. Ask the intended users to perform a short payment simulation: take a note, place it in a slot, reach for coins, close the drawer and hand over change. Observe turning, bending and stepping rather than simply asking whether the layout “looks fine.” A user may adapt silently to an awkward arrangement that becomes tiring across an entire shift.

Keep public and staff routes clear. A drawer should not swing or slide into a queue, emergency route or path used by a person with mobility equipment. If your workplace has applicable accessibility or safety requirements, have the arrangement assessed by an appropriate person. This guide cannot determine compliance from a sketch. The purpose of the mock-up is to discover practical questions before installation, not to replace a formal assessment where one is needed.

Choose front access with the cashier's reach in mind

The usual cashier position matters more than perfect visual symmetry. Place the till where the worker can see its compartments and reach them while maintaining awareness of the transaction. A drawer centered under a screen may force the cashier to stand too far from the customer-facing area. A drawer at the side may be easier to reach but collide with a neighboring worker. Evaluate the whole motion rather than choosing a mounting position from a photograph.

Include users of different heights and those who work seated. An opening that is manageable from one position may be difficult from another. Observe whether note clips require an awkward wrist angle, whether the rear slots are visible and whether the tray can close without the cashier moving their knees. If a configuration cannot reasonably accommodate intended users, reconsider the furniture, mounting method or workflow instead of assuming training will solve a physical mismatch.

Keep the transaction staging area separate. The customer may need a place for a purse, phone, purchase or receipt; that area should not sit over the drawer's moving path or encourage reaching into the till. Avoid presenting a generic drawer layout as a security design. For access policy and key custody, use the shared-checkout guide, and seek a suitable security assessment for your premises. Physical convenience and controlled access need to be considered together.

Treat mounting as a structural decision

Under-counter installation can free the work surface, but it transfers loads to the counter and brackets. Follow the manufacturer's approved mounting instructions, including permitted fasteners and clearances. Ask the counter owner or installer whether the substrate can support the arrangement. Thin board, hollow surfaces and damaged material may require a different approach. Do not infer strength from the thickness visible at the front edge; the load-bearing structure can be different underneath.

Consider both static weight and repeated movement. The drawer contains money and an insert, and opening or closing introduces forces through the mounting points. A loosely fitted bracket can shift even if the empty drawer seemed stable during installation. Inspect any fitted system according to the manufacturer's maintenance directions. If it loosens, stop using the affected arrangement and obtain an authorized repair rather than tightening an unknown fastener indefinitely.

Do not drill blindly. Confirm what is behind the proposed fixing point, including electrical wiring, plumbing or other equipment. Installation work should be done by a competent authorized person. Also preserve access for normal removal and service. A beautiful enclosed fit is unhelpful if replacing the approved cable requires cutting furniture apart. Plan how an authorized technician will reach the mounting screws, connector and identification label after the counter is fully equipped.

Route cables for service rather than concealment

A tidy cable route should avoid strain, pinch points, heat, cleaning chemicals and contact with the moving tray. Use appropriate support and enough slack for the permitted movement, without leaving a loose loop where feet or stocked goods can catch it. Keep power leads and the drawer connection identifiable. Do not bind cables so tightly that inspection or replacement becomes difficult. Follow the equipment instructions for bend limits and connector handling.

The printer and drawer may need to be serviced separately. Give each connection a private label or diagram so a replacement printer does not lead to an accidental port swap. A modular-looking drawer socket must not be confused with a network socket. See the connection-path guide before ordering any cable or adapter. Physical fit never establishes electrical compatibility, and a cable that reaches a socket is not necessarily the correct cable for it.

Leave a documented inspection point. The opening-shift check should be able to identify an obstruction or visibly strained lead without dismantling equipment or reaching into hazardous areas. A cashier should not be expected to repair mounting or wiring. The opening-shift routine distinguishes a visible readiness check from technical servicing, while the care guide describes how to stop and report a problem without making it worse.

Allow the till to be counted and removed deliberately

If you choose a removable insert, find out how removal is performed and what clearance it requires. It may need more vertical space than ordinary opening. Check whether clips, a lock position or another permitted action is involved. The ability to lift the insert in an empty showroom does not prove that it can be lifted from a recess under your counter. Simulate the movement with an empty compatible insert where possible and authorized.

Decide where the insert goes after removal. A controlled counting location needs sufficient surface area, privacy appropriate to your policy and a way to avoid mixing tills. Do not treat a nearby shelf as secure merely because it is behind the counter. When several inserts are used, identify each one consistently and keep its custody record separate. The closing-count guide explains why an insert movement needs a documented cash movement rather than an informal swap.

Keep media slots in perspective. They may allow slips or certain items to be inserted while the main drawer is closed, but they do not guarantee separation or secure storage. Find out where inserted items land and how they are retrieved. If notes deposited through a slot enter the count later, the closing procedure needs to account for them. A hidden compartment is only useful when people know its purpose and routinely inspect it under an authorized process.

Finish with an installation acceptance walk-through

Once the equipment is installed, repeat the original simulation before normal trading. Open the drawer through the supported path, observe the full movement and check for contact with furniture or cables. Ask more than one intended user to reach every compartment and close it normally. Check that keys and approved service points remain accessible to authorized staff, without leaving them openly available to everyone. Record unresolved issues instead of treating installation day as an automatic acceptance.

Include neighboring tasks in the walk-through. Scan an item, tear a receipt, place a bag, process a hypothetical return and clean the adjacent surface using approved methods. A layout can pass a simple opening test but fail once daily materials are present. Do not store loose supplies above the tray where they can drop into the mechanism. Give each frequent object a place so the drawer does not become a spare shelf between transactions.

Retain the final sketch and mounting information with the checkout inventory. Note any limits, such as a required clear zone or permitted seating position, in the workplace procedure. When furniture or equipment later moves, reassess the arrangement rather than inheriting old approval. A well-fitted drawer supports the operation quietly; it does not demand special contortions, undocumented cable tricks or constant clearing before each sale.
