Share a drawer without sharing the ambiguity

Define custody, point-of-sale permissions, key access and shift handovers when several people use one cash station.

Mara Ellis · fictional editorial persona
Published 11 October 2026 · desk-based guidance

Practical decisions for a cash checkout.

The complete overviewMethod and limitationsContact availability

A shared cash drawer is often a sensible response to limited counter space or overlapping duties. The difficulty is not that several people can open the same box; it is that the physical money, the software record and the worker's responsibility can become disconnected. A name attached to a transaction does not prove that only that person touched the till. A key opening can bypass an electronic event. Clear custody and exception rules make those limits visible rather than pretending that the hardware resolves them.

This page offers a hypothetical workflow framework, not a security certification or employment policy. The publication's editor is fictional and the material does not claim hands-on evaluation. Adapt it to authorized business controls and obtain appropriate professional advice for your premises. Begin with the checkout planning article for the overall arrangement, then use this guide to define what “shared” means at one particular station.

Choose a custody model people can explain

There are several possible models. A till may belong to one cashier for a shift, to a defined team at a station, or to a time period that ends with a documented handover. Each has trade-offs. Individual custody can make responsibility clearer but require more inserts or more frequent counts. Station custody can reduce interruptions but make a discrepancy harder to associate with one person's actions. A time-based model needs a real boundary, not merely a timetable.

State the selected model in plain language. Who accepts the opening amount? Who may handle cash during the period? Who approves transfers or refunds? Who closes the record? If the answer is “the manager,” identify the role actually available during trading and the alternative when that person is absent. A policy that depends on a worker who is never present at peak time will be replaced by informal habits, whether or not it looks good on paper.

Do not overstate what the model proves. Even a individually assigned till can be accessed by a manager, key holder or another worker under an exception. Document those permitted events. The point of assignment is to make custody and access intelligible, not to create automatic blame for every shortage. A closing discrepancy is a reason to follow the approved review process, not evidence by itself that the assigned person caused it.

Separate software identity from physical access

Use individual accounts or identifiers where your point-of-sale system and policy support them. Assign permissions to actual duties, including refunds, no-sale openings and report access. Do not give every cashier broad manager rights merely to avoid asking for authorization. Conversely, avoid a rule that prevents a worker from completing ordinary duties without constant interruptions. Permission design should match the operation and be reviewed when roles change.

A software event tells you what the configured system records, not everything that happened at the drawer. Mechanical keys, manual cash movements and unattended access can sit outside that record. Learn which actions your particular system logs and how reports identify them. Do not claim that a drawer-opening event is a complete audit trail unless the documentation and configuration actually support that claim. A printed receipt is not proof of exclusive physical custody either.

Shared logins make attribution weaker and can expose credentials unnecessarily. Follow the business's authentication rules and never put passwords, access codes or recovery details on a public-facing counter note. Keep the operational instruction separate from the secret used to access the system. The connection-path guide identifies which device triggers opening; this guide identifies who is authorized to request that action and what the record can reasonably tell you afterward.

Give keys their own custody rule

Treat drawer keys as a separate access route. Identify who holds them, when they can be used and how exceptional openings are documented. A spare left in an unlocked cup beside the terminal may make a printer outage convenient, but it also gives anyone near the station a mechanical route into the drawer. The appropriate storage and access arrangement depends on the premises and business policy, not on a generic recommendation about hiding a key.

Do not publish key numbers or describe a bypass method in public documentation. Keep replacement information in the approved private inventory where authorized staff can obtain it. If a key is missing, follow the business's incident and replacement procedure. Do not assume a found look-alike key is suitable or force a cylinder. Locks and keys should be handled according to the manufacturer's instructions and any applicable security policy.

Train the difference between ordinary electronic opening and authorized manual access. A new cashier should know whom to ask if the drawer remains shut, not simply where a spare is concealed. Manual access should not be used to disguise a failed transaction or repeatedly reopen a drawer without a business reason. The care guide explains safe observation when equipment does not respond, while the outage procedure should explain how trading continues, if it is permitted to continue.

Make shift change a real boundary

Decide whether a handover requires a count, an acknowledged prepared insert or another approved method. Record the station, till identity, outgoing role, incoming role and time. If both people count together, make clear what they are acknowledging: the observed physical amount, not an unqualified guarantee about earlier transactions. A handover record is strongest when it describes a specific event that another person could recognize from the surrounding records.

Keep transactions from straddling the boundary without a rule. A customer refund started by one cashier and completed by another can otherwise land in an unclear period. Resolve or explicitly transfer the active transaction under policy before changing custody. In a location that cannot pause, use the approved parallel station or spare-insert process. A silent insert swap is not a documented handover, even when the people involved believe they remember what happened.

Give the incoming worker a known opening position. The opening-shift guide covers counting and denomination readiness, while the cash-close guide covers the outgoing reconciliation. These procedures meet at the handover but are not interchangeable. A closing total can include money designated for removal; an opening float is the amount accepted for the next period. Label those amounts so the incoming worker does not inherit a misunderstood number.

Handle temporary cover without abandoning responsibility

Short breaks and brief task changes can create more ambiguity than formal shift changes. Decide whether another worker may handle the till while the assigned cashier is away, how that access is recorded and whether a handover is required. The rule should be proportionate and usable. A procedure so burdensome that everyone skips it does not become effective merely because it is strict; an informal “help yourself” rule leaves little information to review.

Cover should not require borrowing the absent person's credentials. Use the system's supported identity and permissions for the covering worker. If the software cannot represent the chosen custody model adequately, document the limitation and choose an approved operational control rather than assuming a log will provide information it does not contain. Ask the responsible manager or adviser to review the arrangement where accountability requirements are significant.

Plan unexpected departures too. A worker may become ill or need to leave immediately. Identify who can secure or take custody of the station, what count is required and how unresolved transactions are recorded. Do not make an emergency handover depend on the departing person's memory or willingness to finish a detailed investigation. Preserve observations and use the defined escalation route. Clear exceptions protect both the business record and the people involved.

Keep cash movements attached to their reason

Refunds, paid-outs, change deliveries and cash removed from the station need authorized records with an identifiable purpose. A worker should not put an unsigned note in the till and assume it will be understood at close. Use the business's approved process and obtain approval where required. Explain whether the point-of-sale report already includes the movement so the closing worker does not apply it again in a separate calculation.

An exchange between drawers is still a movement even if nobody calls it a sale. Record it using the appropriate procedure, identifying both stations when required. Moving change during a rush without documentation can create an apparent shortage at one till and an overage at another. The fact that the combined money balances does not tell each cashier what their own expected amount should be. Coordination is useful only when it leaves a traceable record.

Keep unrelated money separate. Tips, personal cash, petty cash and sales receipts may follow different accounting and custody rules. Do not use a spare compartment as permission to mix them. If the business collects another category of money at the same station, define its physical location and record method explicitly. An organized insert can support that separation, but it cannot determine the accounting treatment or who is authorized to remove the contents.

Review the arrangement without turning logs into accusations

Use recurring operational problems as a reason to review the procedure: unexplained no-sale access, frequent change transfers, handovers that lack counts, or a queue that prevents the assigned worker from keeping custody. These observations may indicate a mismatch between staffing, layout and controls. They should prompt a careful review of records and training, not an automatic conclusion about a person's intent. Follow fair and lawful business policies when investigating discrepancies.

Physical layout can improve the routine without claiming to solve security. The counter-fit guide helps keep the open drawer from crossing another worker's route and gives inserts a deliberate removal path. A counter where everyone must lean over the same till invites accidental interference. Consider whether a separate station, a different mounting position or a clearer transaction staging area would reduce that overlap.

Review roles when staff, software or equipment changes. Remove access that is no longer needed under the authorized process, update the private inventory and train the revised handover. Do not assume a new device inherits the old permission configuration or that a new manager understands the key rule. A shared checkout can be dependable when its limits are explicit: known custody, appropriate permissions, controlled key access and documented exceptions. The aim is clarity, not an illusion that a single drawer can prove everything about a shift.
